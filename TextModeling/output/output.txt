5. Introduction   
Agriculture plays a fundamental role in society, yet traditional farming 
practices often lead to waste and unsustainable resource use. The Circular 
Economy (CE) is an approach that seeks to break this cycle by promoting 
recycling, waste reduction, and the creation of value from byproducts. This 
project aims to investigate how the circular economy can be implemented in 
agriculture, demonstrating the benefits of this approach and discussing the 
potential challenges.  
 
The document will explore how agricultural practices can be restructured to 
align with circular economy principles, offering a detailed strategy that 
encompasses every stage of farming, from soil preparation to harvesting and 
beyond. Through the application of  CE methods, we can decrease 
environmental impact while creating a more robust and efficient agricultural 
system.  
 
Additionally, the project will propose a five -year plan to guide the 
implementation of circular economy practices in agriculture, complete wi th 
specific objectives and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to measure success. 
The alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs) will also be discussed, showing how the circular economy in 
agriculture can contribute to broader gl obal sustainability  efforts.

8. Linear Economy and Its drawback s: 
Linear  Economy in agriculture is a  new-aged  traditional method and approach 
towards farming, it works on the principle of  the “take-make -and-dispose ” 
model. It requires a lot of resources to run these models  leading  to the 
generation of waste through different stages/ processes  in agriculture . It le ads

to environmental damage, and soil degradation and is not eco -friendly     
making  it not a good and sustainable process for the future increase of Food 
consumption and less waste generation  (Sariatli, 2017) . 
Characteristics in Linear Economy:  
1. Resource extraction: We need raw materials from outside such as 
fertilizers, pesticides, etc. 
2. High Input use: More synthetic inputs  and inorganic inputs are given to 
the soil for different uses like Pests , and unwanted herbs.  
3. Single -use resources: The used resources are thrown away, they either get 
filled up landfills or if not incinerated release  a lot of pollution and dust 
into the air and leave  unwanted ash too.  This might include crop residues, 
packaging, and other agricultural byproducts.  
4. Waste  generation is very high in a Linear Economical way  
 
Environmental Impacts through the following Linear Economy: This leads to 
soil degradation, polluting land, water and soil. It Leads to contamination of 
resources and this also increases Carbon emission into the atmosphere.  
Additionally, the disposal of agricultural waste contributes to pollution  
Economic Challenges: This increases the annual budget for the farmers  
through expensive inorganic yet unwanted materials being injected into the 
soil. The high r esource use and waste generation in a linear system also raise 
sustainability concerns, as these practices are not conducive to long -term 
productivity and resilience.   
 
9. Circular  Economy and Its Principles : 
A circular economy (CE) is a sustainability -focused approach that seeks to 
reduce waste by reusing and recycling resources, creating a closed -loop 
system. Unlike the traditional linear economy's "take -make -dispose" model, 
CE aims to keep materials in use f or as long as possible. In agriculture, this 
means turning waste into valuable resources like compost or bioenergy, 
minimizing the need for new inputs, and promoting practices that regenerate 
the soil. This approach benefits both the environment and the ec onomy by  

Page | 12  
  
reducing pollution, lowering costs, and supporting resource 
efficiency. (Velasco -Muñoz et al., 2021) .

sustainability  Finally,  To Create a regenerative system that enables  the closure 
of nutrient  loops and minimizes  leakages.  
Implementation of CE principles in Agriculture :  
1. Production and Planning: Should know the knowledge of the demand 
for food and resources.  
2.  Production organization: Correct  way of growing crops and no negative 
impacts on the crops to increase yield and less wastage.  
3. Production application: Planting , harvesting, and evaluation of possible 
errors throughout  the process .

11. Circular  Economy and its challenges  and overcoming it : 
A good and futuristic model too has some disadvantages and challenges , and 
we will discuss them. We will also discuss the strategies to minimize those 
challenges.  
The Circular Economy (CE) model face s several hurdles. Regulatory 
constraints often limit how much companies can embrace circular practices. 
Optimizing reverse logistics —where materials are returned for recycling or 
reuse —can be complex and costly. The wide geographic spread of businesses 
complicates efficient material collection and redistribution. Additionally, 
defining system boundaries is tricky, leading to potential leakage where 
materials escape the circular loop. These challenges require innovative 
solutions and collaborative efforts to realize the benefits of a circular economy 
fully.

To overcome the challenges in implementing the Circular Economy (CE), 
consider these strategies:  
Maximizing Resource Utilization : By narrowing resource loops, you ensure 
that every resource is used to its fullest potential. This involves optimizing 
agricultural practices to reduce waste and make the most of existing resources.  
Extending Product Lifespan : Slowing resource loops means extending the 
life of agricultural products and equipment. This strateg y emphasizes 
maintenance, repair, and repurposing, allowing farmers to use their      
resources longer and reduce the need for new inputs.  
Recycling and Reusing Materials : Closing resource loops involves creating 
systems where agricultural materials are r ecycled and reused, minimizing 
waste. This can include composting organic waste, reusing equipment, and 
repurposing byproducts.  
Regenerating Resource Flows : Regeneration focuses on restoring resources 
to their natural state. This strategy emphasizes practi ces like composting and 
cover cropping, which help regenerate soil health and support sustainable 
farming.

 14. Key Performance Indicators (KPI)  
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) for implementing Circular Economy (CE) in 
agriculture should focus on resource efficiency, waste reduction, recycling, and 
overall sustainability. Based on the summarized thread, here are some new KPIs 
along with their calculation methods:   
KPI 1: Waste Reduction  
 Description : Measures the reduction in agricultural waste generated over 
time.  
 Calculation : Compare the total weight  or volume of waste before and after 
implementing CE practices. Express this as a percentage reduction.  
KPI 2: Resource Recovery Rate  
 Description : Assesses the proportion of waste that is recycled,                  
reused, or composted.  
 Calculation : Divid e the total amount of waste that is recycled, reused, or 
composted by the total waste generated. Express this as a percentage.  
KPI 3: Fertilizer Efficiency  
 Description : Measures the efficiency of fertilizer use.  
 Calculation : Compare the amount of fertilize r used with crop yield. 
Calculate the ratio of fertilizer input (kg) to crop output (tons) to determine 
efficiency.  
KPI 4: Water Usage Efficiency  
 Description : Evaluate  the efficiency of water use in irrigation systems.  
 Calculation : Measure the amount of water used per hectare and compare it 
to crop yield. A lower ratio indicates greater efficiency.  
KPI 5: Energy Usage Reduction  
 Description : Assesses the reduction in energy use through CE practices.  
 Calculation : Track energy consumption before and after im plementing CE 
measures. Calculate the percentage reduction in energy use.  
 

Page | 18  
 KPI 6: Bioenergy Production  
 Description : Measures the amount of energy generated from agricultural 
waste.  
 Calculation : Determine the total energy output from bioenergy sources like 
biogas. Express this in kilowatt -hours (kWh) or megajoules (MJ).  
KPI 7: Soil Health Improvement  
 Description : Assesses changes in soil health and fertility.  
 Calculation : Use soil tests to measure organic matter content, pH, and 
nutrient levels. Track change s over time to evaluate improvement.  
KPI 8: Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction  
 Description : Evaluate  the reduction in greenhouse gas emissions.  
 Calculation : Measure emissions before and after CE            
implementation. Express the reduction as a percentage decrease in      
carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e).  
KPI 9: Farm Revenue from Circular Practices  
 Description : Measures the revenue generated from circular economy 
activities.  
 Calculation : Calculate the i ncome from activities like selling compost, 
bioenergy, or recycled products. Express this as a percentage of total farm 
revenue.  
 
15. Complementary Crops in Crop Rotation : 
Complementary crops in crop rotation involve alternating different types of 
crops in  a strategic sequence to boost soil health, manage nutrients, and curb 
pests and diseases. This approach combines the strengths of traditional crop 
rotation with the benefits of companion planting, fostering a more sustainable 
agricultural system.  
 
Crop rotation involves growing different crops on the same field over several 
seasons to prevent soil exhaustion and disrupt pest cycles. Complementary  

Page | 19  
  
crops add an extra layer by considering how various  plants can work together 
to improve each other's g rowth. Benefits include improved soil fertility, natural 
pest control, reduced weed growth, and better resource use.  
 
1. Corn and Soybeans  
Corn Waste: Corn stalks and cobs can be used as mulch or composted to add 
organic matter to the soil.  
Benefit for Soy beans: The organic matter from corn waste improves soil 
structure and fertility, supporting soybean growth.  
2. Wheat and Barley  
Wheat Waste: Wheat straw can be used as animal bedding or mulch, and can 
be composted to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Barley: T he composted wheat straw adds nutrients to the            
soil, benefiting barley, which also has similar nutrient requirements.   
3. Beans and Corn  
Bean Waste: Bean plants fix nitrogen in the soil, and their waste can be 
composted or used as green manure.  
Benefit for Corn: The added nitrogen from bean waste helps improve soil 
fertility for corn, which is a heavy feeder.  
4. Rice and Vegetables  
Rice Waste: Rice straw can be composted or used as mulch to improve soil 
structure and moisture retention.  
Benefit f or Vegetables: The organic matter from rice waste benefits leafy

5. Introduction   
Agriculture plays a fundamental role in society, yet traditional farming 
practices often lead to waste and unsustainable resource use. The Circular 
Economy (CE) is an approach that seeks to break this cycle by promoting 
recycling, waste reduction, and the creation of value from byproducts. This 
project aims to investigate how the circular economy can be implemented in 
agriculture, demonstrating the benefits of this approach and discussing the 
potential challenges.  
 
The document will explore how agricultural practices can be restructured to 
align with circular economy principles, offering a detailed strategy that 
encompasses every stage of farming, from soil preparation to harvesting and 
beyond. Through the application of  CE methods, we can decrease 
environmental impact while creating a more robust and efficient agricultural 
system.  
 
Additionally, the project will propose a five -year plan to guide the 
implementation of circular economy practices in agriculture, complete wi th 
specific objectives and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to measure success. 
The alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs) will also be discussed, showing how the circular economy in 
agriculture can contribute to broader gl obal sustainability  efforts.

towards farming, it works on the principle of  the “take-make -and-dispose ” 
model. It requires a lot of resources to run these models  leading  to the 
generation of waste through different stages/ processes  in agriculture . It le ads

to environmental damage, and soil degradation and is not eco -friendly     
making  it not a good and sustainable process for the future increase of Food

Characteristics in Linear Economy:  
1. Resource extraction: We need raw materials from outside such as 
fertilizers, pesticides, etc. 
2. High Input use: More synthetic inputs  and inorganic inputs are given to 
the soil for different uses like Pests , and unwanted herbs.  
3. Single -use resources: The used resources are thrown away, they either get

Additionally, the disposal of agricultural waste contributes to pollution  
Economic Challenges: This increases the annual budget for the farmers  
through expensive inorganic yet unwanted materials being injected into the 
soil. The high r esource use and waste generation in a linear system also raise 
sustainability concerns, as these practices are not conducive to long -term 
productivity and resilience.   
 
9. Circular  Economy and Its Principles : 
A circular economy (CE) is a sustainability -focused approach that seeks to 
reduce waste by reusing and recycling resources, creating a closed -loop 
system. Unlike the traditional linear economy's "take -make -dispose" model, 
CE aims to keep materials in use f or as long as possible. In agriculture, this 
means turning waste into valuable resources like compost or bioenergy, 
minimizing the need for new inputs, and promoting practices that regenerate 
the soil. This approach benefits both the environment and the ec onomy by  

Page | 12  
  
reducing pollution, lowering costs, and supporting resource 
efficiency. (Velasco -Muñoz et al., 2021) . 
3 Principles of CE in agriculture : 
Firstly , 1. Efficient Use of resources , 2. Optimization of process ,                      
3. Prevent wastage Secondly,  1. Environmental,  2. Economic,  1. Social 
sustainability  Finally,  To Create a regenerative system that enables  the closure 
of nutrient  loops and minimizes  leakages.  
Implementation of CE principles in Agriculture :  
1. Production and Planning: Should know the knowledge of the demand 
for food and resources.  
2.  Production organization: Correct  way of growing crops and no negative 
impacts on the crops to increase yield and less wastage.  
3. Production application: Planting , harvesting, and evaluation of possible 
errors throughout  the process .

11. Circular  Economy and its challenges  and overcoming it : 
A good and futuristic model too has some disadvantages and challenges , and 
we will discuss them. We will also discuss the strategies to minimize those 
challenges.  
The Circular Economy (CE) model face s several hurdles. Regulatory 
constraints often limit how much companies can embrace circular practices. 
Optimizing reverse logistics —where materials are returned for recycling or 
reuse —can be complex and costly. The wide geographic spread of businesses 
complicates efficient material collection and redistribution. Additionally, 
defining system boundaries is tricky, leading to potential leakage where 
materials escape the circular loop. These challenges require innovative 
solutions and collaborative efforts to realize the benefits of a circular economy 
fully.

To overcome the challenges in implementing the Circular Economy (CE), 
consider these strategies:  
Maximizing Resource Utilization : By narrowing resource loops, you ensure 
that every resource is used to its fullest potential. This involves optimizing 
agricultural practices to reduce waste and make the most of existing resources.  
Extending Product Lifespan : Slowing resource loops means extending the 
life of agricultural products and equipment. This strateg y emphasizes 
maintenance, repair, and repurposing, allowing farmers to use their      
resources longer and reduce the need for new inputs.  
Recycling and Reusing Materials : Closing resource loops involves creating 
systems where agricultural materials are r ecycled and reused, minimizing 
waste. This can include composting organic waste, reusing equipment, and 
repurposing byproducts.  
Regenerating Resource Flows : Regeneration focuses on restoring resources 
to their natural state. This strategy emphasizes practi ces like composting and 
cover cropping, which help regenerate soil health and support sustainable 
farming.

used both qualitative and quantitative methods. The qualitative approach 
centered on field visits, interviews, and surveys with farmers in Medak district, 
Telangana, providing insigh ts into their agricultural techniques, challenges, 
and views on sustainable practices. We sought to understand farmers' 
perspectives on waste management, crop rotation, and livestock practices, 
while also exploring the socio -economic factors that influence  their decisions.  
 
On the quantitative side, we gathered and analyzed data on farming operations, 
including resource usage, crop yields, waste production, and energy 
consumption. This data collection helped us establish a baseline for

comparison and asse ss the effectiveness of CE strategies over time. By 
blending qualitative observations with quantitative data, our goal was to create 
a well -rounded picture of the difficulties and prospects in adopting Circular 
Economy approaches in agriculture.

 14. Key Performance Indicators (KPI)  
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) for implementing Circular Economy (CE) in 
agriculture should focus on resource efficiency, waste reduction, recycling, and 
overall sustainability. Based on the summarized thread, here are some new KPIs 
along with their calculation methods:   
KPI 1: Waste Reduction  
 Description : Measures the reduction in agricultural waste generated over 
time.  
 Calculation : Compare the total weight  or volume of waste before and after 
implementing CE practices. Express this as a percentage reduction.  
KPI 2: Resource Recovery Rate  
 Description : Assesses the proportion of waste that is recycled,                  
reused, or composted.  
 Calculation : Divid e the total amount of waste that is recycled, reused, or 
composted by the total waste generated. Express this as a percentage.  
KPI 3: Fertilizer Efficiency  
 Description : Measures the efficiency of fertilizer use.  
 Calculation : Compare the amount of fertilize r used with crop yield. 
Calculate the ratio of fertilizer input (kg) to crop output (tons) to determine 
efficiency.  
KPI 4: Water Usage Efficiency  
 Description : Evaluate  the efficiency of water use in irrigation systems.  
 Calculation : Measure the amount of water used per hectare and compare it 
to crop yield. A lower ratio indicates greater efficiency.  
KPI 5: Energy Usage Reduction  
 Description : Assesses the reduction in energy use through CE practices.  
 Calculation : Track energy consumption before and after im plementing CE 
measures. Calculate the percentage reduction in energy use.  
 

Page | 18  
 KPI 6: Bioenergy Production  
 Description : Measures the amount of energy generated from agricultural 
waste.  
 Calculation : Determine the total energy output from bioenergy sources like 
biogas. Express this in kilowatt -hours (kWh) or megajoules (MJ).  
KPI 7: Soil Health Improvement  
 Description : Assesses changes in soil health and fertility.  
 Calculation : Use soil tests to measure organic matter content, pH, and 
nutrient levels. Track change s over time to evaluate improvement.  
KPI 8: Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction  
 Description : Evaluate  the reduction in greenhouse gas emissions.  
 Calculation : Measure emissions before and after CE            
implementation. Express the reduction as a percentage decrease in      
carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e).  
KPI 9: Farm Revenue from Circular Practices  
 Description : Measures the revenue generated from circular economy 
activities.  
 Calculation : Calculate the i ncome from activities like selling compost, 
bioenergy, or recycled products. Express this as a percentage of total farm 
revenue.  
 
15. Complementary Crops in Crop Rotation : 
Complementary crops in crop rotation involve alternating different types of 
crops in  a strategic sequence to boost soil health, manage nutrients, and curb 
pests and diseases. This approach combines the strengths of traditional crop 
rotation with the benefits of companion planting, fostering a more sustainable 
agricultural system.  
 
Crop rotation involves growing different crops on the same field over several 
seasons to prevent soil exhaustion and disrupt pest cycles. Complementary  

Page | 19  
  
crops add an extra layer by considering how various  plants can work together 
to improve each other's g rowth. Benefits include improved soil fertility, natural 
pest control, reduced weed growth, and better resource use.

organic matter to the soil.  
Benefit for Soy beans: The organic matter from corn waste improves soil 
structure and fertility, supporting soybean growth.  
2. Wheat and Barley  
Wheat Waste: Wheat straw can be used as animal bedding or mulch, and can 
be composted to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Barley: T he composted wheat straw adds nutrients to the

3. Beans and Corn  
Bean Waste: Bean plants fix nitrogen in the soil, and their waste can be 
composted or used as green manure.  
Benefit for Corn: The added nitrogen from bean waste helps improve soil 
fertility for corn, which is a heavy feeder.  
4. Rice and Vegetables  
Rice Waste: Rice straw can be composted or used as mulch to improve soil 
structure and moisture retention.  
Benefit f or Vegetables: The organic matter from rice waste benefits leafy 
greens, tomatoes, and other vegetables, providing a sustainable source of 
nutrients.  
5. Potatoes and Carrots  
Potato Waste: Potato plant tops and other organic waste can be composted or 
used a s green manure.

composted and used to enrich the soil.

Lettuce Waste: Lettuce leaves and stems can be composted or used as green

Squash Waste: Squash vines and other organic waste can be

second cycle. This rotation helps maintain soil health and provides diversity.

CE Strategies : Use composting to enhance soil health, rotate complementary 
crops, and utilize cover crops during off -season to reduce nutrient loss and 
control weeds.  
Year 2: Expand Crop Diversity and Implement Sustainable Irrigation  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Plant rice and lentils for the first

KPIs to Track : Water  Usage Efficiency, Energy Usage Reduction, 
Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction.

to maintain soil health.  
Year 3: Enhance Resource Recovery and Focus on Bioenergy 
Production

first cycle, followed by maize and sorghum for the second cycle. This rotation 
supports resource recovery and promotes soi l structure.  
Off-Season Crops : Plant legumes like lupine or field peas to increase nitrogen 
in the soil during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Resource Recovery Rate, Bioenergy Production, Farm 
Revenue from Circular Practices.  
CE Strategies : Convert crop re sidues into bioenergy, compost organic waste, 
and promote resource recovery by reusing agricultural byproducts.

5. Introduction   
Agriculture plays a fundamental role in society, yet traditional farming 
practices often lead to waste and unsustainable resource use. The Circular 
Economy (CE) is an approach that seeks to break this cycle by promoting 
recycling, waste reduction, and the creation of value from byproducts. This 
project aims to investigate how the circular economy can be implemented in 
agriculture, demonstrating the benefits of this approach and discussing the 
potential challenges.  
 
The document will explore how agricultural practices can be restructured to 
align with circular economy principles, offering a detailed strategy that 
encompasses every stage of farming, from soil preparation to harvesting and 
beyond. Through the application of  CE methods, we can decrease 
environmental impact while creating a more robust and efficient agricultural 
system.  
 
Additionally, the project will propose a five -year plan to guide the 
implementation of circular economy practices in agriculture, complete wi th 
specific objectives and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to measure success. 
The alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs) will also be discussed, showing how the circular economy in 
agriculture can contribute to broader gl obal sustainability  efforts.

Linear  Economy in agriculture is a  new-aged  traditional method and approach 
towards farming, it works on the principle of  the “take-make -and-dispose ” 
model. It requires a lot of resources to run these models  leading  to the 
generation of waste through different stages/ processes  in agriculture . It le ads

to environmental damage, and soil degradation and is not eco -friendly     
making  it not a good and sustainable process for the future increase of Food 
consumption and less waste generation  (Sariatli, 2017) . 
Characteristics in Linear Economy:  
1. Resource extraction: We need raw materials from outside such as 
fertilizers, pesticides, etc. 
2. High Input use: More synthetic inputs  and inorganic inputs are given to 
the soil for different uses like Pests , and unwanted herbs.  
3. Single -use resources: The used resources are thrown away, they either get

Economic Challenges: This increases the annual budget for the farmers  
through expensive inorganic yet unwanted materials being injected into the 
soil. The high r esource use and waste generation in a linear system also raise 
sustainability concerns, as these practices are not conducive to long -term 
productivity and resilience.   
 
9. Circular  Economy and Its Principles : 
A circular economy (CE) is a sustainability -focused approach that seeks to 
reduce waste by reusing and recycling resources, creating a closed -loop 
system. Unlike the traditional linear economy's "take -make -dispose" model, 
CE aims to keep materials in use f or as long as possible. In agriculture, this 
means turning waste into valuable resources like compost or bioenergy, 
minimizing the need for new inputs, and promoting practices that regenerate 
the soil. This approach benefits both the environment and the ec onomy by  

Page | 12  
  
reducing pollution, lowering costs, and supporting resource 
efficiency. (Velasco -Muñoz et al., 2021) . 
3 Principles of CE in agriculture :

3. Prevent wastage Secondly,  1. Environmental,  2. Economic,  1. Social 
sustainability  Finally,  To Create a regenerative system that enables  the closure 
of nutrient  loops and minimizes  leakages.  
Implementation of CE principles in Agriculture :  
1. Production and Planning: Should know the knowledge of the demand 
for food and resources.  
2.  Production organization: Correct  way of growing crops and no negative 
impacts on the crops to increase yield and less wastage.  
3. Production application: Planting , harvesting, and evaluation of possible 
errors throughout  the process .

11. Circular  Economy and its challenges  and overcoming it : 
A good and futuristic model too has some disadvantages and challenges , and 
we will discuss them. We will also discuss the strategies to minimize those 
challenges.  
The Circular Economy (CE) model face s several hurdles. Regulatory 
constraints often limit how much companies can embrace circular practices. 
Optimizing reverse logistics —where materials are returned for recycling or 
reuse —can be complex and costly. The wide geographic spread of businesses 
complicates efficient material collection and redistribution. Additionally, 
defining system boundaries is tricky, leading to potential leakage where 
materials escape the circular loop. These challenges require innovative 
solutions and collaborative efforts to realize the benefits of a circular economy 
fully.

To overcome the challenges in implementing the Circular Economy (CE), 
consider these strategies:  
Maximizing Resource Utilization : By narrowing resource loops, you ensure 
that every resource is used to its fullest potential. This involves optimizing 
agricultural practices to reduce waste and make the most of existing resources.  
Extending Product Lifespan : Slowing resource loops means extending the 
life of agricultural products and equipment. This strateg y emphasizes 
maintenance, repair, and repurposing, allowing farmers to use their      
resources longer and reduce the need for new inputs.  
Recycling and Reusing Materials : Closing resource loops involves creating 
systems where agricultural materials are r ecycled and reused, minimizing 
waste. This can include composting organic waste, reusing equipment, and 
repurposing byproducts.  
Regenerating Resource Flows : Regeneration focuses on restoring resources 
to their natural state. This strategy emphasizes practi ces like composting and 
cover cropping, which help regenerate soil health and support sustainable 
farming.

 14. Key Performance Indicators (KPI)  
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) for implementing Circular Economy (CE) in 
agriculture should focus on resource efficiency, waste reduction, recycling, and 
overall sustainability. Based on the summarized thread, here are some new KPIs 
along with their calculation methods:   
KPI 1: Waste Reduction  
 Description : Measures the reduction in agricultural waste generated over 
time.  
 Calculation : Compare the total weight  or volume of waste before and after 
implementing CE practices. Express this as a percentage reduction.  
KPI 2: Resource Recovery Rate  
 Description : Assesses the proportion of waste that is recycled,                  
reused, or composted.  
 Calculation : Divid e the total amount of waste that is recycled, reused, or 
composted by the total waste generated. Express this as a percentage.  
KPI 3: Fertilizer Efficiency  
 Description : Measures the efficiency of fertilizer use.  
 Calculation : Compare the amount of fertilize r used with crop yield. 
Calculate the ratio of fertilizer input (kg) to crop output (tons) to determine 
efficiency.  
KPI 4: Water Usage Efficiency  
 Description : Evaluate  the efficiency of water use in irrigation systems.  
 Calculation : Measure the amount of water used per hectare and compare it 
to crop yield. A lower ratio indicates greater efficiency.  
KPI 5: Energy Usage Reduction  
 Description : Assesses the reduction in energy use through CE practices.  
 Calculation : Track energy consumption before and after im plementing CE 
measures. Calculate the percentage reduction in energy use.  
 

Page | 18  
 KPI 6: Bioenergy Production  
 Description : Measures the amount of energy generated from agricultural 
waste.  
 Calculation : Determine the total energy output from bioenergy sources like 
biogas. Express this in kilowatt -hours (kWh) or megajoules (MJ).  
KPI 7: Soil Health Improvement  
 Description : Assesses changes in soil health and fertility.  
 Calculation : Use soil tests to measure organic matter content, pH, and 
nutrient levels. Track change s over time to evaluate improvement.  
KPI 8: Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction  
 Description : Evaluate  the reduction in greenhouse gas emissions.  
 Calculation : Measure emissions before and after CE            
implementation. Express the reduction as a percentage decrease in      
carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e).  
KPI 9: Farm Revenue from Circular Practices  
 Description : Measures the revenue generated from circular economy 
activities.  
 Calculation : Calculate the i ncome from activities like selling compost, 
bioenergy, or recycled products. Express this as a percentage of total farm 
revenue.  
 
15. Complementary Crops in Crop Rotation : 
Complementary crops in crop rotation involve alternating different types of 
crops in  a strategic sequence to boost soil health, manage nutrients, and curb 
pests and diseases. This approach combines the strengths of traditional crop 
rotation with the benefits of companion planting, fostering a more sustainable 
agricultural system.  
 
Crop rotation involves growing different crops on the same field over several 
seasons to prevent soil exhaustion and disrupt pest cycles. Complementary  

Page | 19  
  
crops add an extra layer by considering how various  plants can work together 
to improve each other's g rowth. Benefits include improved soil fertility, natural 
pest control, reduced weed growth, and better resource use.  
 
1. Corn and Soybeans  
Corn Waste: Corn stalks and cobs can be used as mulch or composted to add 
organic matter to the soil.  
Benefit for Soy beans: The organic matter from corn waste improves soil 
structure and fertility, supporting soybean growth.  
2. Wheat and Barley  
Wheat Waste: Wheat straw can be used as animal bedding or mulch, and can 
be composted to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Barley: T he composted wheat straw adds nutrients to the            
soil, benefiting barley, which also has similar nutrient requirements.   
3. Beans and Corn  
Bean Waste: Bean plants fix nitrogen in the soil, and their waste can be 
composted or used as green manure.  
Benefit for Corn: The added nitrogen from bean waste helps improve soil 
fertility for corn, which is a heavy feeder.  
4. Rice and Vegetables  
Rice Waste: Rice straw can be composted or used as mulch to improve soil 
structure and moisture retention.  
Benefit f or Vegetables: The organic matter from rice waste benefits leafy 
greens, tomatoes, and other vegetables, providing a sustainable source of 
nutrients.  
5. Potatoes and Carrots  
Potato Waste: Potato plant tops and other organic waste can be composted or 
used a s green manure.  

Page | 20  
  
Benefit for Carrots: The composted potato waste adds organic matter to the 
soil, supporting the growth of carrots, which benefit from well -structured soil.  
6. Tomatoes and Peppers  
Tomato Waste: Tomato plants produce a lot of organic waste,  which can be 
composted and used to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Peppers: The composted tomato waste provides nutrients and 
organic matter to support pepper growth.  
7. Lettuce and Cabbage  
Lettuce Waste: Lettuce leaves and stems can be composted or used as green 
manure.  
Benefit for Cabbage: The organic matter from composted lettuce waste 
benefits cabbage by improving soil structure and nutrient content.  
8. Squash and Beans  
Squash Waste: Squash vines and other organic waste can be               
composted or used as mulch.  
Benefit for Beans: The composted squash waste provides nutrients and organic 
matter, supporting bean growth and soil health.  
9. Cucumbers and Spinach  
Cucumber Waste: Cucumber vines and leaves can be composted to create 
nutrient -rich compost.  
Benefit for Spinach: The composted cucumber waste adds organic matter to 
the soil, enhancing spinach growth.  
 
17. Integrated CE Business Model :  
Year 1: Start with Diverse Crop Rotation and Focus on Soil Health  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Begin with wheat and legumes 
(e.g., chickpeas) for the first cycle, followed by maize and sunflowers for the 
second cycle. This rotation helps maintain soil health and provides diversity.  

Page | 21  
  
Off-Season Crops : Plant cover crops such as clover or ryegrass to p revent soil 
erosion and add organic matter during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Fertilizer Efficiency, Soil Health Improvement, Waste 
Reduction, Resource Recovery Rate.  
CE Strategies : Use composting to enhance soil health, rotate complementary 
crops, and utilize cover crops during off -season to reduce nutrient loss and 
control weeds.  
Year 2: Expand Crop Diversity and Implement Sustainable Irrigation  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Plant rice and lentils for the first 
cycle, followed by wheat and soy beans for the second cycle. This combination 
promotes nitrogen fixation and reduces the need for synthetic fertilizers.  
Off-Season Crops : Use green manure crops like vetch or alfalfa to add 
nutrients to the soil during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Water  Usage Efficiency, Energy Usage Reduction, 
Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction.   
CE Strategies : Optimize irrigation with drip systems to reduce water use, 
promote energy efficiency through renewable sources, and use green manure 
to maintain soil health.  
Year 3: Enhance Resource Recovery and Focus on Bioenergy 
Production  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Rotate wheat and oats for the 
first cycle, followed by maize and sorghum for the second cycle. This rotation 
supports resource recovery and promotes soi l structure.  
Off-Season Crops : Plant legumes like lupine or field peas to increase nitrogen 
in the soil during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Resource Recovery Rate, Bioenergy Production, Farm 
Revenue from Circular Practices.  
CE Strategies : Convert crop re sidues into bioenergy, compost organic waste, 
and promote resource recovery by reusing agricultural byproducts.  

Page | 22  
  
Year 4: Foster Partnerships and Engage in Community Outreach  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Plant barley and beans for the 
first cycle,  followed by wheat and sunflower for the second cycle. This 
rotation increases biodiversity and provides a varied range of crops.  
Off-Season Crops : Use mustard or buckwheat to suppress weeds and improve 
soil structure during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Partnerships Established, Job Creation and Rural 
Development, Community Engagement.  
CE Strategies : Establish partnerships with local businesses, engage in 
community outreach, and continue resource recovery practices to reduce 
waste.  
Year 5: Advocate for Circular Economy Policies and Focus on 
Continuous Improvement  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Rotate rice and              
groundnut for the first cycle, followed by wheat and canola for the           
second cycle. This promotes oilseed production and improves resource 
efficiency.  
Off-Season Crops : Plant cover crops like rye or crimson clover to maintain 
soil health during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Waste Reduction, Resource Recovery Rate, Partnerships 
Established, Farm Revenue from Circular Pra ctices.  
CE Strategies : Advocate for CE policies, focus on continuous improvement 
based on KPIs, and continue promoting off -season cover crops to enhance 
sustainability.

5. Introduction   
Agriculture plays a fundamental role in society, yet traditional farming 
practices often lead to waste and unsustainable resource use. The Circular 
Economy (CE) is an approach that seeks to break this cycle by promoting 
recycling, waste reduction, and the creation of value from byproducts. This 
project aims to investigate how the circular economy can be implemented in 
agriculture, demonstrating the benefits of this approach and discussing the 
potential challenges.  
 
The document will explore how agricultural practices can be restructured to 
align with circular economy principles, offering a detailed strategy that 
encompasses every stage of farming, from soil preparation to harvesting and 
beyond. Through the application of  CE methods, we can decrease 
environmental impact while creating a more robust and efficient agricultural 
system.  
 
Additionally, the project will propose a five -year plan to guide the 
implementation of circular economy practices in agriculture, complete wi th 
specific objectives and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to measure success. 
The alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs) will also be discussed, showing how the circular economy in 
agriculture can contribute to broader gl obal sustainability  efforts.

9. Circular  Economy and Its Principles : 
A circular economy (CE) is a sustainability -focused approach that seeks to 
reduce waste by reusing and recycling resources, creating a closed -loop 
system. Unlike the traditional linear economy's "take -make -dispose" model, 
CE aims to keep materials in use f or as long as possible. In agriculture, this 
means turning waste into valuable resources like compost or bioenergy, 
minimizing the need for new inputs, and promoting practices that regenerate 
the soil. This approach benefits both the environment and the ec onomy by  

Page | 12  
  
reducing pollution, lowering costs, and supporting resource 
efficiency. (Velasco -Muñoz et al., 2021) .

of nutrient  loops and minimizes  leakages.  
Implementation of CE principles in Agriculture :  
1. Production and Planning: Should know the knowledge of the demand 
for food and resources.  
2.  Production organization: Correct  way of growing crops and no negative 
impacts on the crops to increase yield and less wastage.

errors throughout  the process .

11. Circular  Economy and its challenges  and overcoming it : 
A good and futuristic model too has some disadvantages and challenges , and 
we will discuss them. We will also discuss the strategies to minimize those 
challenges.  
The Circular Economy (CE) model face s several hurdles. Regulatory 
constraints often limit how much companies can embrace circular practices. 
Optimizing reverse logistics —where materials are returned for recycling or 
reuse —can be complex and costly. The wide geographic spread of businesses 
complicates efficient material collection and redistribution. Additionally, 
defining system boundaries is tricky, leading to potential leakage where 
materials escape the circular loop. These challenges require innovative 
solutions and collaborative efforts to realize the benefits of a circular economy 
fully.

To overcome the challenges in implementing the Circular Economy (CE), 
consider these strategies:  
Maximizing Resource Utilization : By narrowing resource loops, you ensure 
that every resource is used to its fullest potential. This involves optimizing 
agricultural practices to reduce waste and make the most of existing resources.  
Extending Product Lifespan : Slowing resource loops means extending the 
life of agricultural products and equipment. This strateg y emphasizes 
maintenance, repair, and repurposing, allowing farmers to use their      
resources longer and reduce the need for new inputs.  
Recycling and Reusing Materials : Closing resource loops involves creating 
systems where agricultural materials are r ecycled and reused, minimizing 
waste. This can include composting organic waste, reusing equipment, and 
repurposing byproducts.  
Regenerating Resource Flows : Regeneration focuses on restoring resources 
to their natural state. This strategy emphasizes practi ces like composting and 
cover cropping, which help regenerate soil health and support sustainable 
farming.

 14. Key Performance Indicators (KPI)  
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) for implementing Circular Economy (CE) in 
agriculture should focus on resource efficiency, waste reduction, recycling, and 
overall sustainability. Based on the summarized thread, here are some new KPIs 
along with their calculation methods:   
KPI 1: Waste Reduction  
 Description : Measures the reduction in agricultural waste generated over 
time.  
 Calculation : Compare the total weight  or volume of waste before and after 
implementing CE practices. Express this as a percentage reduction.  
KPI 2: Resource Recovery Rate  
 Description : Assesses the proportion of waste that is recycled,                  
reused, or composted.  
 Calculation : Divid e the total amount of waste that is recycled, reused, or 
composted by the total waste generated. Express this as a percentage.  
KPI 3: Fertilizer Efficiency  
 Description : Measures the efficiency of fertilizer use.  
 Calculation : Compare the amount of fertilize r used with crop yield. 
Calculate the ratio of fertilizer input (kg) to crop output (tons) to determine 
efficiency.  
KPI 4: Water Usage Efficiency  
 Description : Evaluate  the efficiency of water use in irrigation systems.  
 Calculation : Measure the amount of water used per hectare and compare it 
to crop yield. A lower ratio indicates greater efficiency.  
KPI 5: Energy Usage Reduction  
 Description : Assesses the reduction in energy use through CE practices.  
 Calculation : Track energy consumption before and after im plementing CE 
measures. Calculate the percentage reduction in energy use.  
 

Page | 18  
 KPI 6: Bioenergy Production  
 Description : Measures the amount of energy generated from agricultural 
waste.  
 Calculation : Determine the total energy output from bioenergy sources like 
biogas. Express this in kilowatt -hours (kWh) or megajoules (MJ).  
KPI 7: Soil Health Improvement  
 Description : Assesses changes in soil health and fertility.  
 Calculation : Use soil tests to measure organic matter content, pH, and 
nutrient levels. Track change s over time to evaluate improvement.  
KPI 8: Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction  
 Description : Evaluate  the reduction in greenhouse gas emissions.  
 Calculation : Measure emissions before and after CE            
implementation. Express the reduction as a percentage decrease in      
carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e).  
KPI 9: Farm Revenue from Circular Practices  
 Description : Measures the revenue generated from circular economy 
activities.  
 Calculation : Calculate the i ncome from activities like selling compost, 
bioenergy, or recycled products. Express this as a percentage of total farm 
revenue.  
 
15. Complementary Crops in Crop Rotation : 
Complementary crops in crop rotation involve alternating different types of 
crops in  a strategic sequence to boost soil health, manage nutrients, and curb 
pests and diseases. This approach combines the strengths of traditional crop 
rotation with the benefits of companion planting, fostering a more sustainable 
agricultural system.  
 
Crop rotation involves growing different crops on the same field over several 
seasons to prevent soil exhaustion and disrupt pest cycles. Complementary  

Page | 19  
  
crops add an extra layer by considering how various  plants can work together 
to improve each other's g rowth. Benefits include improved soil fertility, natural 
pest control, reduced weed growth, and better resource use.  
 
1. Corn and Soybeans  
Corn Waste: Corn stalks and cobs can be used as mulch or composted to add 
organic matter to the soil.  
Benefit for Soy beans: The organic matter from corn waste improves soil 
structure and fertility, supporting soybean growth.  
2. Wheat and Barley  
Wheat Waste: Wheat straw can be used as animal bedding or mulch, and can 
be composted to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Barley: T he composted wheat straw adds nutrients to the            
soil, benefiting barley, which also has similar nutrient requirements.   
3. Beans and Corn  
Bean Waste: Bean plants fix nitrogen in the soil, and their waste can be 
composted or used as green manure.  
Benefit for Corn: The added nitrogen from bean waste helps improve soil 
fertility for corn, which is a heavy feeder.  
4. Rice and Vegetables  
Rice Waste: Rice straw can be composted or used as mulch to improve soil 
structure and moisture retention.  
Benefit f or Vegetables: The organic matter from rice waste benefits leafy 
greens, tomatoes, and other vegetables, providing a sustainable source of 
nutrients.  
5. Potatoes and Carrots  
Potato Waste: Potato plant tops and other organic waste can be composted or 
used a s green manure.  

Page | 20  
  
Benefit for Carrots: The composted potato waste adds organic matter to the 
soil, supporting the growth of carrots, which benefit from well -structured soil.  
6. Tomatoes and Peppers  
Tomato Waste: Tomato plants produce a lot of organic waste,  which can be 
composted and used to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Peppers: The composted tomato waste provides nutrients and 
organic matter to support pepper growth.  
7. Lettuce and Cabbage  
Lettuce Waste: Lettuce leaves and stems can be composted or used as green 
manure.  
Benefit for Cabbage: The organic matter from composted lettuce waste 
benefits cabbage by improving soil structure and nutrient content.  
8. Squash and Beans  
Squash Waste: Squash vines and other organic waste can be               
composted or used as mulch.  
Benefit for Beans: The composted squash waste provides nutrients and organic 
matter, supporting bean growth and soil health.  
9. Cucumbers and Spinach  
Cucumber Waste: Cucumber vines and leaves can be composted to create 
nutrient -rich compost.  
Benefit for Spinach: The composted cucumber waste adds organic matter to 
the soil, enhancing spinach growth.

to maintain soil health.

Production

KPIs to Track : Resource Recovery Rate, Bioenergy Production, Farm

5. Introduction   
Agriculture plays a fundamental role in society, yet traditional farming 
practices often lead to waste and unsustainable resource use. The Circular 
Economy (CE) is an approach that seeks to break this cycle by promoting 
recycling, waste reduction, and the creation of value from byproducts. This 
project aims to investigate how the circular economy can be implemented in 
agriculture, demonstrating the benefits of this approach and discussing the 
potential challenges.  
 
The document will explore how agricultural practices can be restructured to 
align with circular economy principles, offering a detailed strategy that 
encompasses every stage of farming, from soil preparation to harvesting and 
beyond. Through the application of  CE methods, we can decrease 
environmental impact while creating a more robust and efficient agricultural 
system.  
 
Additionally, the project will propose a five -year plan to guide the 
implementation of circular economy practices in agriculture, complete wi th 
specific objectives and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to measure success. 
The alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs) will also be discussed, showing how the circular economy in 
agriculture can contribute to broader gl obal sustainability  efforts.

9. Circular  Economy and Its Principles : 
A circular economy (CE) is a sustainability -focused approach that seeks to 
reduce waste by reusing and recycling resources, creating a closed -loop 
system. Unlike the traditional linear economy's "take -make -dispose" model, 
CE aims to keep materials in use f or as long as possible. In agriculture, this 
means turning waste into valuable resources like compost or bioenergy, 
minimizing the need for new inputs, and promoting practices that regenerate 
the soil. This approach benefits both the environment and the ec onomy by  

Page | 12  
  
reducing pollution, lowering costs, and supporting resource 
efficiency. (Velasco -Muñoz et al., 2021) .

sustainability  Finally,  To Create a regenerative system that enables  the closure 
of nutrient  loops and minimizes  leakages.  
Implementation of CE principles in Agriculture :  
1. Production and Planning: Should know the knowledge of the demand 
for food and resources.  
2.  Production organization: Correct  way of growing crops and no negative 
impacts on the crops to increase yield and less wastage.  
3. Production application: Planting , harvesting, and evaluation of possible 
errors throughout  the process .

11. Circular  Economy and its challenges  and overcoming it : 
A good and futuristic model too has some disadvantages and challenges , and 
we will discuss them. We will also discuss the strategies to minimize those 
challenges.  
The Circular Economy (CE) model face s several hurdles. Regulatory 
constraints often limit how much companies can embrace circular practices. 
Optimizing reverse logistics —where materials are returned for recycling or 
reuse —can be complex and costly. The wide geographic spread of businesses 
complicates efficient material collection and redistribution. Additionally, 
defining system boundaries is tricky, leading to potential leakage where 
materials escape the circular loop. These challenges require innovative 
solutions and collaborative efforts to realize the benefits of a circular economy 
fully.

To overcome the challenges in implementing the Circular Economy (CE), 
consider these strategies:  
Maximizing Resource Utilization : By narrowing resource loops, you ensure 
that every resource is used to its fullest potential. This involves optimizing 
agricultural practices to reduce waste and make the most of existing resources.  
Extending Product Lifespan : Slowing resource loops means extending the 
life of agricultural products and equipment. This strateg y emphasizes 
maintenance, repair, and repurposing, allowing farmers to use their      
resources longer and reduce the need for new inputs.  
Recycling and Reusing Materials : Closing resource loops involves creating 
systems where agricultural materials are r ecycled and reused, minimizing 
waste. This can include composting organic waste, reusing equipment, and 
repurposing byproducts.  
Regenerating Resource Flows : Regeneration focuses on restoring resources 
to their natural state. This strategy emphasizes practi ces like composting and 
cover cropping, which help regenerate soil health and support sustainable 
farming.

 14. Key Performance Indicators (KPI)  
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) for implementing Circular Economy (CE) in 
agriculture should focus on resource efficiency, waste reduction, recycling, and 
overall sustainability. Based on the summarized thread, here are some new KPIs 
along with their calculation methods:   
KPI 1: Waste Reduction  
 Description : Measures the reduction in agricultural waste generated over 
time.  
 Calculation : Compare the total weight  or volume of waste before and after 
implementing CE practices. Express this as a percentage reduction.  
KPI 2: Resource Recovery Rate

15. Complementary Crops in Crop Rotation : 
Complementary crops in crop rotation involve alternating different types of 
crops in  a strategic sequence to boost soil health, manage nutrients, and curb 
pests and diseases. This approach combines the strengths of traditional crop 
rotation with the benefits of companion planting, fostering a more sustainable 
agricultural system.  
 
Crop rotation involves growing different crops on the same field over several 
seasons to prevent soil exhaustion and disrupt pest cycles. Complementary  

Page | 19  
  
crops add an extra layer by considering how various  plants can work together 
to improve each other's g rowth. Benefits include improved soil fertility, natural 
pest control, reduced weed growth, and better resource use.  
 
1. Corn and Soybeans  
Corn Waste: Corn stalks and cobs can be used as mulch or composted to add 
organic matter to the soil.  
Benefit for Soy beans: The organic matter from corn waste improves soil 
structure and fertility, supporting soybean growth.  
2. Wheat and Barley  
Wheat Waste: Wheat straw can be used as animal bedding or mulch, and can 
be composted to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Barley: T he composted wheat straw adds nutrients to the            
soil, benefiting barley, which also has similar nutrient requirements.   
3. Beans and Corn  
Bean Waste: Bean plants fix nitrogen in the soil, and their waste can be 
composted or used as green manure.  
Benefit for Corn: The added nitrogen from bean waste helps improve soil 
fertility for corn, which is a heavy feeder.  
4. Rice and Vegetables  
Rice Waste: Rice straw can be composted or used as mulch to improve soil 
structure and moisture retention.  
Benefit f or Vegetables: The organic matter from rice waste benefits leafy 
greens, tomatoes, and other vegetables, providing a sustainable source of 
nutrients.  
5. Potatoes and Carrots  
Potato Waste: Potato plant tops and other organic waste can be composted or 
used a s green manure.

Benefit for Carrots: The composted potato waste adds organic matter to the 
soil, supporting the growth of carrots, which benefit from well -structured soil.  
6. Tomatoes and Peppers  
Tomato Waste: Tomato plants produce a lot of organic waste,  which can be 
composted and used to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Peppers: The composted tomato waste provides nutrients and 
organic matter to support pepper growth.  
7. Lettuce and Cabbage  
Lettuce Waste: Lettuce leaves and stems can be composted or used as green 
manure.  
Benefit for Cabbage: The organic matter from composted lettuce waste 
benefits cabbage by improving soil structure and nutrient content.  
8. Squash and Beans  
Squash Waste: Squash vines and other organic waste can be

9. Cucumbers and Spinach

crops, and utilize cover crops during off -season to reduce nutrient loss and 
control weeds.

KPIs to Track : Water  Usage Efficiency, Energy Usage Reduction,

to maintain soil health.

Production

in the soil during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Resource Recovery Rate, Bioenergy Production, Farm 
Revenue from Circular Practices.

5. Introduction   
Agriculture plays a fundamental role in society, yet traditional farming 
practices often lead to waste and unsustainable resource use. The Circular 
Economy (CE) is an approach that seeks to break this cycle by promoting 
recycling, waste reduction, and the creation of value from byproducts. This 
project aims to investigate how the circular economy can be implemented in 
agriculture, demonstrating the benefits of this approach and discussing the 
potential challenges.  
 
The document will explore how agricultural practices can be restructured to 
align with circular economy principles, offering a detailed strategy that 
encompasses every stage of farming, from soil preparation to harvesting and 
beyond. Through the application of  CE methods, we can decrease 
environmental impact while creating a more robust and efficient agricultural 
system.  
 
Additionally, the project will propose a five -year plan to guide the 
implementation of circular economy practices in agriculture, complete wi th 
specific objectives and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to measure success. 
The alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs) will also be discussed, showing how the circular economy in 
agriculture can contribute to broader gl obal sustainability  efforts.

8. Linear Economy and Its drawback s: 
Linear  Economy in agriculture is a  new-aged  traditional method and approach 
towards farming, it works on the principle of  the “take-make -and-dispose ” 
model. It requires a lot of resources to run these models  leading  to the 
generation of waste through different stages/ processes  in agriculture . It le ads  

Page | 11  
  
to environmental damage, and soil degradation and is not eco -friendly     
making  it not a good and sustainable process for the future increase of Food 
consumption and less waste generation  (Sariatli, 2017) . 
Characteristics in Linear Economy:  
1. Resource extraction: We need raw materials from outside such as 
fertilizers, pesticides, etc. 
2. High Input use: More synthetic inputs  and inorganic inputs are given to 
the soil for different uses like Pests , and unwanted herbs.  
3. Single -use resources: The used resources are thrown away, they either get 
filled up landfills or if not incinerated release  a lot of pollution and dust 
into the air and leave  unwanted ash too.  This might include crop residues, 
packaging, and other agricultural byproducts.  
4. Waste  generation is very high in a Linear Economical way  
 
Environmental Impacts through the following Linear Economy: This leads to 
soil degradation, polluting land, water and soil. It Leads to contamination of 
resources and this also increases Carbon emission into the atmosphere.  
Additionally, the disposal of agricultural waste contributes to pollution  
Economic Challenges: This increases the annual budget for the farmers  
through expensive inorganic yet unwanted materials being injected into the 
soil. The high r esource use and waste generation in a linear system also raise 
sustainability concerns, as these practices are not conducive to long -term 
productivity and resilience.   
 
9. Circular  Economy and Its Principles : 
A circular economy (CE) is a sustainability -focused approach that seeks to 
reduce waste by reusing and recycling resources, creating a closed -loop 
system. Unlike the traditional linear economy's "take -make -dispose" model, 
CE aims to keep materials in use f or as long as possible. In agriculture, this 
means turning waste into valuable resources like compost or bioenergy, 
minimizing the need for new inputs, and promoting practices that regenerate 
the soil. This approach benefits both the environment and the ec onomy by

reducing pollution, lowering costs, and supporting resource 
efficiency. (Velasco -Muñoz et al., 2021) .

11. Circular  Economy and its challenges  and overcoming it : 
A good and futuristic model too has some disadvantages and challenges , and 
we will discuss them. We will also discuss the strategies to minimize those 
challenges.  
The Circular Economy (CE) model face s several hurdles. Regulatory 
constraints often limit how much companies can embrace circular practices. 
Optimizing reverse logistics —where materials are returned for recycling or 
reuse —can be complex and costly. The wide geographic spread of businesses 
complicates efficient material collection and redistribution. Additionally, 
defining system boundaries is tricky, leading to potential leakage where 
materials escape the circular loop. These challenges require innovative 
solutions and collaborative efforts to realize the benefits of a circular economy 
fully.

To overcome the challenges in implementing the Circular Economy (CE), 
consider these strategies:  
Maximizing Resource Utilization : By narrowing resource loops, you ensure 
that every resource is used to its fullest potential. This involves optimizing 
agricultural practices to reduce waste and make the most of existing resources.  
Extending Product Lifespan : Slowing resource loops means extending the 
life of agricultural products and equipment. This strateg y emphasizes 
maintenance, repair, and repurposing, allowing farmers to use their      
resources longer and reduce the need for new inputs.  
Recycling and Reusing Materials : Closing resource loops involves creating 
systems where agricultural materials are r ecycled and reused, minimizing 
waste. This can include composting organic waste, reusing equipment, and 
repurposing byproducts.  
Regenerating Resource Flows : Regeneration focuses on restoring resources 
to their natural state. This strategy emphasizes practi ces like composting and 
cover cropping, which help regenerate soil health and support sustainable 
farming.  
 
12. Method ology  
To thoroughly evaluate Circular Economy (CE) practices in agriculture, we 
used both qualitative and quantitative methods. The qualitative approach 
centered on field visits, interviews, and surveys with farmers in Medak district, 
Telangana, providing insigh ts into their agricultural techniques, challenges, 
and views on sustainable practices. We sought to understand farmers' 
perspectives on waste management, crop rotation, and livestock practices, 
while also exploring the socio -economic factors that influence  their decisions.  
 
On the quantitative side, we gathered and analyzed data on farming operations, 
including resource usage, crop yields, waste production, and energy 
consumption. This data collection helped us establish a baseline for

comparison and asse ss the effectiveness of CE strategies over time. By 
blending qualitative observations with quantitative data, our goal was to create 
a well -rounded picture of the difficulties and prospects in adopting Circular 
Economy approaches in agriculture.

 14. Key Performance Indicators (KPI)  
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) for implementing Circular Economy (CE) in 
agriculture should focus on resource efficiency, waste reduction, recycling, and 
overall sustainability. Based on the summarized thread, here are some new KPIs 
along with their calculation methods:   
KPI 1: Waste Reduction  
 Description : Measures the reduction in agricultural waste generated over 
time.  
 Calculation : Compare the total weight  or volume of waste before and after 
implementing CE practices. Express this as a percentage reduction.  
KPI 2: Resource Recovery Rate  
 Description : Assesses the proportion of waste that is recycled,                  
reused, or composted.  
 Calculation : Divid e the total amount of waste that is recycled, reused, or 
composted by the total waste generated. Express this as a percentage.  
KPI 3: Fertilizer Efficiency  
 Description : Measures the efficiency of fertilizer use.  
 Calculation : Compare the amount of fertilize r used with crop yield. 
Calculate the ratio of fertilizer input (kg) to crop output (tons) to determine 
efficiency.  
KPI 4: Water Usage Efficiency  
 Description : Evaluate  the efficiency of water use in irrigation systems.  
 Calculation : Measure the amount of water used per hectare and compare it 
to crop yield. A lower ratio indicates greater efficiency.  
KPI 5: Energy Usage Reduction  
 Description : Assesses the reduction in energy use through CE practices.  
 Calculation : Track energy consumption before and after im plementing CE 
measures. Calculate the percentage reduction in energy use.  
 

Page | 18  
 KPI 6: Bioenergy Production  
 Description : Measures the amount of energy generated from agricultural 
waste.  
 Calculation : Determine the total energy output from bioenergy sources like 
biogas. Express this in kilowatt -hours (kWh) or megajoules (MJ).  
KPI 7: Soil Health Improvement  
 Description : Assesses changes in soil health and fertility.  
 Calculation : Use soil tests to measure organic matter content, pH, and 
nutrient levels. Track change s over time to evaluate improvement.  
KPI 8: Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction  
 Description : Evaluate  the reduction in greenhouse gas emissions.  
 Calculation : Measure emissions before and after CE            
implementation. Express the reduction as a percentage decrease in      
carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e).  
KPI 9: Farm Revenue from Circular Practices  
 Description : Measures the revenue generated from circular economy 
activities.  
 Calculation : Calculate the i ncome from activities like selling compost, 
bioenergy, or recycled products. Express this as a percentage of total farm 
revenue.  
 
15. Complementary Crops in Crop Rotation : 
Complementary crops in crop rotation involve alternating different types of 
crops in  a strategic sequence to boost soil health, manage nutrients, and curb 
pests and diseases. This approach combines the strengths of traditional crop 
rotation with the benefits of companion planting, fostering a more sustainable 
agricultural system.  
 
Crop rotation involves growing different crops on the same field over several 
seasons to prevent soil exhaustion and disrupt pest cycles. Complementary  

Page | 19  
  
crops add an extra layer by considering how various  plants can work together 
to improve each other's g rowth. Benefits include improved soil fertility, natural 
pest control, reduced weed growth, and better resource use.  
 
1. Corn and Soybeans  
Corn Waste: Corn stalks and cobs can be used as mulch or composted to add 
organic matter to the soil.  
Benefit for Soy beans: The organic matter from corn waste improves soil 
structure and fertility, supporting soybean growth.  
2. Wheat and Barley  
Wheat Waste: Wheat straw can be used as animal bedding or mulch, and can 
be composted to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Barley: T he composted wheat straw adds nutrients to the            
soil, benefiting barley, which also has similar nutrient requirements.   
3. Beans and Corn  
Bean Waste: Bean plants fix nitrogen in the soil, and their waste can be 
composted or used as green manure.  
Benefit for Corn: The added nitrogen from bean waste helps improve soil 
fertility for corn, which is a heavy feeder.  
4. Rice and Vegetables  
Rice Waste: Rice straw can be composted or used as mulch to improve soil 
structure and moisture retention.  
Benefit f or Vegetables: The organic matter from rice waste benefits leafy 
greens, tomatoes, and other vegetables, providing a sustainable source of 
nutrients.  
5. Potatoes and Carrots  
Potato Waste: Potato plant tops and other organic waste can be composted or 
used a s green manure.  

Page | 20  
  
Benefit for Carrots: The composted potato waste adds organic matter to the 
soil, supporting the growth of carrots, which benefit from well -structured soil.  
6. Tomatoes and Peppers  
Tomato Waste: Tomato plants produce a lot of organic waste,  which can be 
composted and used to enrich the soil.  
Benefit for Peppers: The composted tomato waste provides nutrients and 
organic matter to support pepper growth.  
7. Lettuce and Cabbage  
Lettuce Waste: Lettuce leaves and stems can be composted or used as green 
manure.  
Benefit for Cabbage: The organic matter from composted lettuce waste 
benefits cabbage by improving soil structure and nutrient content.  
8. Squash and Beans  
Squash Waste: Squash vines and other organic waste can be               
composted or used as mulch.  
Benefit for Beans: The composted squash waste provides nutrients and organic 
matter, supporting bean growth and soil health.  
9. Cucumbers and Spinach  
Cucumber Waste: Cucumber vines and leaves can be composted to create 
nutrient -rich compost.  
Benefit for Spinach: The composted cucumber waste adds organic matter to 
the soil, enhancing spinach growth.  
 
17. Integrated CE Business Model :  
Year 1: Start with Diverse Crop Rotation and Focus on Soil Health  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Begin with wheat and legumes 
(e.g., chickpeas) for the first cycle, followed by maize and sunflowers for the 
second cycle. This rotation helps maintain soil health and provides diversity.  

Page | 21  
  
Off-Season Crops : Plant cover crops such as clover or ryegrass to p revent soil 
erosion and add organic matter during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Fertilizer Efficiency, Soil Health Improvement, Waste 
Reduction, Resource Recovery Rate.  
CE Strategies : Use composting to enhance soil health, rotate complementary 
crops, and utilize cover crops during off -season to reduce nutrient loss and 
control weeds.  
Year 2: Expand Crop Diversity and Implement Sustainable Irrigation  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Plant rice and lentils for the first 
cycle, followed by wheat and soy beans for the second cycle. This combination 
promotes nitrogen fixation and reduces the need for synthetic fertilizers.  
Off-Season Crops : Use green manure crops like vetch or alfalfa to add 
nutrients to the soil during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Water  Usage Efficiency, Energy Usage Reduction, 
Greenhouse Gas Emissions Reduction.   
CE Strategies : Optimize irrigation with drip systems to reduce water use, 
promote energy efficiency through renewable sources, and use green manure 
to maintain soil health.  
Year 3: Enhance Resource Recovery and Focus on Bioenergy 
Production  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Rotate wheat and oats for the 
first cycle, followed by maize and sorghum for the second cycle. This rotation 
supports resource recovery and promotes soi l structure.  
Off-Season Crops : Plant legumes like lupine or field peas to increase nitrogen 
in the soil during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Resource Recovery Rate, Bioenergy Production, Farm 
Revenue from Circular Practices.  
CE Strategies : Convert crop re sidues into bioenergy, compost organic waste, 
and promote resource recovery by reusing agricultural byproducts.  

Page | 22  
  
Year 4: Foster Partnerships and Engage in Community Outreach  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Plant barley and beans for the 
first cycle,  followed by wheat and sunflower for the second cycle. This 
rotation increases biodiversity and provides a varied range of crops.  
Off-Season Crops : Use mustard or buckwheat to suppress weeds and improve 
soil structure during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Partnerships Established, Job Creation and Rural 
Development, Community Engagement.  
CE Strategies : Establish partnerships with local businesses, engage in 
community outreach, and continue resource recovery practices to reduce 
waste.  
Year 5: Advocate for Circular Economy Policies and Focus on 
Continuous Improvement  
Complementary Crops and Crop Rotation : Rotate rice and              
groundnut for the first cycle, followed by wheat and canola for the           
second cycle. This promotes oilseed production and improves resource 
efficiency.  
Off-Season Crops : Plant cover crops like rye or crimson clover to maintain 
soil health during the off -season.  
KPIs to Track : Waste Reduction, Resource Recovery Rate, Partnerships 
Established, Farm Revenue from Circular Pra ctices.  
CE Strategies : Advocate for CE policies, focus on continuous improvement 
based on KPIs, and continue promoting off -season cover crops to enhance 
sustainability.
